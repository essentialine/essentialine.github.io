Okay, this is Egon Schiele — I've never seen this before.
It's a beautiful drawing of a tree, a flower, a plant.
I've never traced of something like this.
There's a lot of detail up top where the flowers are, and 
the bark has this textural, stained-glass quality.
I don't think my tracing this silhouette is going to get anywhere
near this level of beauty, but it'll give me something, the exterior.
I have traced Schiele's famous women many times before, but
not since starting the blog. I had no idea he made this kind
of gorgeous plant work, and found it so interesting — yet
still so recognizably linked to how he draws the lines of the
women, their edges, limbs. It's so striking, yet has the sense
of Schiele — the control of space is so clear and powerful,
just as in his famous works. An autumnal, melancholic quality
immediately comes out at us.

Starting with the negative spaces, like always.
Schiele's precise here — these lines
inside the tree, fibers, I like the wiry-ness of it.
That's what makes this an educational tracing exercise —
it's such an unusual picture.
The branches especially — I love the branches, particularly
on the right, where there's real thickness to them, that
beautiful
angular quality.

There's this long branch or fiber on the left, so special
because the thickness varies so much along its length —
hard to get that with a single fine line, so will need two
lines instead of one.
There seems to be a leaf hanging off the lower-left branch,
or maybe a dangling stem.
And then this chunk of plant at the bottom center, and a bit
more at the upper right — which ties into what I think is
one of the real strengths of this drawing: Schiele's usual
expressivity with white space, the negative space doing as
much as the line.

One confusing bit: the plant in the middle reads clearly,
but that graceful twig on the left, with its varying
thickness, could easily be misread as a wide trunk coming
off the side — which it isn't.
Using two lines there instead of one gets that across
correctly.
The original has these gorgeous shapes throughout the bark
and the leaves, dark orange, and I thought about going back
in with brush pens for that — but I'll leave it.
It came out better, different, than I expected already.
For me it's settled — I feel the pen is not for that. 
Schiele's technique is beautiful, and eventually I may learn in those directions,
but currently I am interested in silhouette,
perspective, white space, emptiness, implicitness.

**Background.** *Pflaumenbaum* ("Plum Tree"), 1909–1910, oil on
canvas, 57 × 50 cm, is an early Egon Schiele landscape/still-life
study — he was about nineteen or twenty, a couple of years before
the figure work he's best known for.
In the Hessisches Landesmuseum Darmstadt (Hessian State Museum
Darmstadt), Germany.
Falls in the period right after Schiele broke from the Vienna
Academy's more conventional training and started developing the
angular, wiry line that would define his later portraits —
visible here already in how the branches and bark are handled,
even in a straightforwardly botanical subject.

**Auction context.** Schiele's auction record is *Häuser mit
bunter Wäsche, Vorstadt II* (Houses with Laundry, Suburb II), a
Krumau cityscape, at £24.7M ($39.6M) at Sotheby's London, June
2011.
That's a major painting, not a close comp for a piece like this.
For scale on smaller/lesser works: *Knabe im Matrosenanzug* (Boy
in a Sailor Suit), a 1914 drawing restituted to the heirs of
Fritz Grünbaum (a cabaret performer whose collection was looted
by the Nazis), sold for £3.3M ($4.2M) at Christie's London,
March 2025 — still well into seven figures, but a different
tier entirely from the cityscape record.
*Pflaumenbaum* itself, an early museum-held work, isn't going
anywhere.
