![My tracing of the waltz sequence, gesture only](/assets/images/muybridge-waltz-line-and-photograph/muybridge-tracing.png)
*My tracing of Eadweard Muybridge's [Animal Locomotion, Plate 195](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Animal_locomotion._Plate_195_(Boston_Public_Library).jpg), "Nude woman dancing a waltz," 1887 — reduced to weighted gesture lines. Source plate linked, not reproduced (see note below).*

This is Muybridge, a woman doing a waltz. I was hesitant, I don't
want to draw so many, but then I thought — this is the whole thing, I can
do a minimum line. So I wondered: what is the minimum line needed to
convey this motion? There's motion here.

Plate 195 is twenty-four exposures of a woman dancing a waltz,
shot from two synchronized camera angles running in parallel down the sheet.
Published by the University of Pennsylvania; this particular scan comes
from the Boston Public Library's Rare Books Department, one of several
institutions holding original prints of the plates.

I thought it's probably good to draw the weighted side. So I traced out a
little bit of what it used to be, with the weighted side. Another
interesting thing is that what we really need is the weighted side and
the minimum line. She seems to have shifted her weight to the other side.
The weight comes from Alon Bement's guidance. He emphasized that the
first and most important line is the one carrying the weight, which
gives a drawing balance and realism, lost if we don't prioritize it.

Just the weighted line — I don't think we're seeing enough to make out
that this is a figure in some of the individual lines, but these are a group.

I don't quite understand what Muybridge was searching for, really —
there's the horse, of course, which I think is amazing, and I'll probably
also do a cat one. Even the horse is flying, as we have motion, the
weight of the being must move, the animal causes its weight to shift and
move, and so tracing teaches the origin and basis.
