I love Vallotton.
Just a sense of form.
Expression, I think, is quite...
It's an unusual portrait, really, I think, and her hat is so expressive.
It's a contrast, inanimate yet large,
and it has a passion that is also duplicated by her strap falling.

And then of course there's dark colors here,
such a sharp rendition of the hat, and then deep anatomical sense.
And then also the fact that it kind of disappears.
This isn't just an image, in a way.
He's trying to convey more than that.
I wouldn't think of trying to say it in words.
Next to the women Manet and Degas painted looking out at us,
Manet is different, more direct,
and Degas perhaps prettier and more atmospheric.

Her shoulder is beautiful.
As you trace it, you realize that this is finely anatomic.
Vallotton executes in a very precise way.
And then here also we're getting just a tremendous perspective.
There's no mistakes here.
Especially this distant feather.
He's not holding back on whatever the largeness of her hat is meant to do.

I love the perspective in her shoulders.
I think his foreshortening is better.
He has more on her near shoulder, and her collarbones,
and I didn't pursue it, perhaps next time,
whereas I put a highlight on her far shoulder
that I think is less important, though more distant.
I also drew a kind of cross contour over her chest
which conflicts with where she is holding her shirt,
and instead makes her appear to be wearing it,
though I think they do convey form,
at least, that I didn't know how to get otherwise.

For her chin, a line here to help understand her face.
Just her jawline, it's a little tricky.
I was wondering how her jawline helps, even though that one's in the shade.
It's like something in the foreground
is helping us understand something in the background.
It partly has to do with the semantics and anatomy.

I felt I couldn't get across her facial form properly with just the silhouette,
since she was kind of scrunching her shoulders,
and I didn't want to do a broad swath on her left side like Vallotton,
since I didn't want to paint.
So I drew her right jawline, and I think it was enough.
Overlaps like that are the only thing we have, actually,
and it was needed to disambiguate where her jaw actually was.
Once I gave only half,
I think our anatomic understanding was satisfied enough
to formalize the rest of her lower face.

Then I realized her left side seems washed out,
which is something I'd been trying to do elsewhere.
Another thing, looking back on this one, I do find ironically that
her dark side is actually what I might call the light side in my tracing.
It's the semantics.
I have only lines, but her hairline I didn't complete,
and so it looks washed out by a bright light.
That is what I was looking for, for example,
[yesterday](/drawing/vallotton__portrait_de_marthe_mellot/) in Ferriss.

## Auction

*Le Chapeau violet* has stayed with the collection Arthur
and Hedy Hahnloser-Bühler built in Winterthur,
now the Hahnloser/Jaeggli Stiftung,
and no auction record for it turned up.

<a id="background"></a>

## Background

Vallotton signed and dated this canvas in 1907,
the year the Hahnlosers began collecting.
On their Paris trip of 1908 the painter Carl Montag introduced them to Vallotton,
and they bought a painting from him,
"the beginning of a lifelong friendship",
says the [Villa Flora](https://digital.kmw.ch/en/villa-flora/about-the-history-of-the-villa-flora/the-history-of-villa-flora).
By 1909 Vallotton wrote that their house, the Flora,
was about to become "a true museum".
The family set up its foundation in 1980,
the villa opened as a museum in 1995,
and after six years with the foundation's works
housed at the Kunstmuseum Bern,
it reopened in 2024 as part of the Kunst Museum Winterthur.
The sitter isn't named.
The painting was shown at the Grand Palais in Paris in *Félix Vallotton,
le feu sous la glace* (fire under the ice), 2013–14.

Vallotton had joined the Nabis in 1892
and made his name with flat black-and-white woodcuts.
By the 1900s he had moved to "highly finished portraits
and nudes".
Here the green wall is almost flat,
and the woman is modelled smoothly under a hat painted as a loud,
broken mass of violet feathers.

A line tracing keeps only edges, so depth has to come from how the edges meet.
Where one contour stops against another,
as the arm crossing her chest cuts off the chemise,
or the far side of the jaw runs into the shadow of the neck,
the eye reads the uninterrupted line as in front.
The shoulders and the slipping chemise are mostly that kind of overlap.
