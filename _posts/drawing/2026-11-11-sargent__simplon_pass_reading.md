So, a Sargent watercolor.
This one I trace with a brush pen and find the essential points.
It has an umbrella, the dress, huge drapery,
sort of study, and then underneath, though,
what's interesting is there's a lot of anatomy, of course,
underneath.
Somehow the way he does this, there's one direction,
but anatomy is kind of there.
And like these umbrellas are, I think,
ultimately a kind of a choice, a stylistic sort of a thing.
Yes, that's good. It even reminds me of the Samothrace.

And then of course here, it's Sargent's just tremendous power of her gaze.
*Las Meninas* and *Olympia* are very much in line with this.
You immediately see a person here,
which is the reason why he's so interesting,
the shading too, and her gazing out at you.
He's with watercolors, and it gives him
tremendous color,
and he's gone all out with it.

One good thing about my brush pen is that, of course,
it can kind of track and follow the way that a brush works.
And so all of these sort of watercolor lines
are sort of executable with this brush pen.
Color is emotion and life.
I lost a lot but kept a kind of abstract
structure and theory of the drawing.

I was thinking, what's the purpose of this huge umbrella?
Obviously, this may be just realism, 
this is how it looks on a warm day in whatever country you might be in.
Obviously a bright day, somewhere, and these dresses are
so spread out, and excessively so.
And just, why?
Again, would just have been the fashion of the time.
I'm not exactly sure what this blue blob is up here.
I think it could be another small parasol.

I think it is realism and the opportunity to add the largeness
in contrast to their faces, which are precise and contrastingly smaller.

Well, it occurred to me,
and I think we can even see it here,
that part of what's going on here is that by painting such large draperies,
and it especially hit me on the umbrella,
he's depicting a huge three-dimensional anchor.
So an umbrella
and a huge dress that are drawn this way give you
a very strong suggestion of an object,
a very large object, that is orienting us,
and causing us to immediately think about this picture in 3D.
The fact that we're orienting it, and I didn't draw the ribs
and mine is worse for it, supports this idea.

That's one thing I think this type of a brush pen is quite good at,
these undulations of black.
I think this is her arm, on maybe a pillow?
And then, on her large dress in the front,
he did put in these sort of large drapery tonal variations.
Again, this is the largest object in our foreground.
And I think these are, again,
just pulling us to think about this whole scene more three-dimensionally than perhaps we otherwise would.

## Auction

The sheet is in the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston,
accession 12.214 ([Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sargent_-_Simplon_Pass_Reading,_about_1911,_12.214.jpg)).
Museums bought Sargent's watercolours in bulk in his lifetime.
In 1909 he showed eighty-six in New York,
and the Brooklyn Museum bought eighty-three of them ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Singer_Sargent)).

## Background

John Singer Sargent was born in Florence in 1856 to American parents,
and died at his home in Chelsea in 1925.
He made roughly 900 oil paintings
and more than 2,000 watercolours ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Singer_Sargent)).
Several summers he went up into the Swiss Alps with his sisters Emily
and Violet and Violet's daughters, Rose-Marie and Reine,
who sat for a number of his pictures between 1906
and 1913 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Singer_Sargent)).
No source I have found names the two women in this sheet.
The Simplon of the title is an Alpine pass,
2,006 metres up between Brig in the Valais
and Domodossola in Piedmont ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplon_Pass)).

Watercolour was where Sargent painted for himself.
Away from the pressures of the portrait studio he painted friends
and family resting in bright landscapes,
with a more vivid palette
and freer handling than his commissions allowed,
and he was fluent in the whole range of the medium,
transparent
and opaque ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Singer_Sargent)).

Watercolour is pigment carried in water,
laid on paper ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watercolor_painting)).
A transparent wash can darken the paper
but never lighten it,
so the brightest whites are the paper itself, left bare.
Here the two dresses are long wet strokes of white, lilac
and blue, with the paper showing through for the sun,
while the faces are small and exact.
