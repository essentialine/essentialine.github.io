<a id="first"></a>

**First sitting.** This is from Leonardo's notebook,
a study for *Leda e il cigno* (Leda and the Swan),
and there's far more hatching here than in Fanciulla.
The edge is fairly clean, so it should trace.
I've traced this one before;
coming back to it I'm more exploratory, more curious,
though I still fall back on my typical approach —
the one that seems most effective for me.
I break the eyebrow, because he did; get a nostril;
start from the center of the head, the indentation, out.
There's a huge amount of hair, braided very carefully —
it must have taken a very long time to do.

The more I looked at it,
the more I saw how anatomically perfect it is;
Leonardo knows his anatomy,
and the broken lines are just a tool for rendering it.
He's emphasizing the depression into the eye socket.
And where Fanciulla was hatched straight — one direction,
left-handed —
here he's hatching *along the contours* of the form,
a different technique, perhaps more common.
I tried to follow it, but I feel there's more to do.

**Second sitting.** Try again.
The last one didn't come out as I wanted —
I didn't follow the contours as correctly as he does —
but the basic contour was okay, so I start from there.
His eyes are three-dimensional.
I track the bones, the collarbone.
I think this one was somewhat better, though too busy.

**Third sitting.** Another attempt,
and this time to use more judgment about the 3D form itself rather than just tracing the edges.
The shoulders are great.
There's a very smooth change of surface —
how do you convey that without just following the outline?
The eye sockets go in more than I had them.
That's probably the best one yet, though it still feels a little unfinished.

Tracing is tracing lines to elucidate and depict form,
which is the shape in 3D.
Tracing lines without knowing the form in 3D leads to a simple
and less believable rendition.
By the third try what I had was just that the form is real,
and that it is what he is capturing;
I have a way of doing it, but I have more to learn from his,
especially the finer, contour-following hatching.

This one I think Leonardo did the face; it seems too subtle.
But on the same page is another one
where the face is just a simplistic placeholder.
Three sittings and none of them finished —
this is something that will probably always hold lessons
and value to trace and draw and observe, the hair
and the face.

**Background.** These are Leonardo's studies for the head of Leda in his lost painting *Leda e il cigno*,
c. 1505–08 — pen and ink, some over black chalk,
all in the Royal Collection Trust at Windsor Castle.

The painting itself is known only through workshop copies;
the most cited is the "Spiridon Leda" in the Galleria degli Uffizi,
Firenze (Florence).
