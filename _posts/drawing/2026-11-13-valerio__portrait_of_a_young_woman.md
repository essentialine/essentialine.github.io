There are a few lines, I think,
that are the finalizations of various stages of earlier searching lines.
There's curly swirl,
these final lines,
a lot of masters do this,
but at the same time, these have just a precision to them,
this very real angularity.

A graphite line can be laid down lightly and pressed harder later,
and the clay in the core sets how dark each pass can go.
But I have to know where I'm going and I have to execute it,
or it can be difficult to correct.
It's not about building up gradually.

The angularity is often in the edges of the fabrics,
and where surfaces are bending or turning.
Valerio was trained by Charlet,
a lithographer and draughtsman of soldiers.

And even her drapery,
it's really quite extraordinarily expressive of the shape,
and then her gaze here.

In Valerio's drawing she looks straight out at us with a small smile,
but on my tracing her lids come down
and her eyes read as lowered or closed.
I specifically don't draw the pupils, as a mask.

We sense the shapes finely.

Her head is tipped to her right and turned a little,
so the line through her eyes
and the line through her mouth are no longer level,
but they stay parallel to each other.
Her jawline is compatible with the side of her face and hair.

I picked this sheet for eye contact.
Well, it's a good technique,
we can see the precision, and the end line is expressive.

The sheet came to The Metropolitan Museum of Art
as the bequest of Harvey Salzman in 2020,
and was entered in 2022 as 2022.25.8 ([The Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/854144)).

## Background

Théodore Valerio was born at Herserange in Lorraine in 1819
and died at Vichy in 1879.
He was a painter, an engraver
and a lithographer ([Wikipédia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%A9odore_Valerio)).
He studied under Nicolas-Toussaint Charlet,
travelled with him through Germany and Italy,
and showed at the Salon for the first time in 1838,
at nineteen ([Wikipédia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%A9odore_Valerio)).
In 1851 and 1852 he was in Hungary and the Balkans,
and in the Crimean War he followed the Ottoman army of Osman Pasha,
drawing the costumes
and customs of the peoples he met in watercolours
and drawings that are close to ethnography.
He showed them at the Paris Exposition universelle of 1855,
and late in life he settled in Brittany ([Wikipédia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%A9odore_Valerio)).

This sheet is graphite on paper, 28.8 by 23.6 centimetres.
The Met dates it 1860 to 1879,
with a question mark ([The Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/854144)).
Her head is worked up in close hatching
and she looks straight out at us, while her shawl
and sleeves below are left in light, open outline.

Graphite is the pencil Valerio's own country made.
When the British blockade cut France off from English graphite,
Nicolas-Jacques Conté found in 1795
that powdered graphite mixed with clay could be formed into rods
and fired in a kiln.
More clay gives a harder core and a paler line,
less clay a softer and darker one,
which is the scale of grades from H to B that artists use still ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pencil)).
