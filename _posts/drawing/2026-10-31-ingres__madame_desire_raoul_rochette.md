I chose a minimal style, with a Pitt FM.
For me to try to do it as close to him as possible,
I reach for my brush pen, but then to understand more cleanly I chose the Pitt FM.

If there's a single line that would help me with the form,
what would be the next one?
What makes one line worth more than another?
Some lines are following or emphasizing other lines,
whereas others are showing entirely new information.

Oh, one hand has a glove and one doesn't,
she's changing,
she is moving between the inside and outside worlds.
See, the way to depict it is this rumpling,
and then, of course, the top back of her hand.
Well, I continue with that, so I draw her gloves.
And then at the lower parts of her dress are some telling lines.
Ingres finishes her face and curls,
and leaves her coat and sleeves in long open lines.
What can a dress tell us that a face can't?
Indirection, the form.
Sometimes we can infer things that are fixed, like skeletal positions,
and then other unseen information.
So, the Pitt kind of gives you some of that brush flex but without too much.
I like it here.

Ingres resented being "the man who draws the little portraits",
and this one he gave to a friend, with no fee.
Why would the minor work outlast the ambition?
Sometimes the minor one is the one that one is freest with,
and so, ironically, that moves most freely and beautifully.

<a id="auction"></a>

## Auction

The sheet has never been sold at auction.
It stayed in the family, from Raoul-Rochette to his widow,
then to her grandson Raoul Perrin, his widow
and their son Edmond,
until Wildenstein & Co. in New York sold it to Cleveland in 1927,
says the [Cleveland Museum of Art](https://clevelandart.org/art/1927.437).

<a id="background"></a>

## Background

The sitter is Antoinette-Claude Houdon (1790–1878),
the youngest daughter of the sculptor Jean-Antoine Houdon.
Ingres gave the drawing to her husband,
the archaeologist Désiré Raoul-Rochette,
and wrote at the lower right "Ingres à son ami et confrere Monsieur Raoul Rochette 1830" ([Cleveland Museum of Art](https://clevelandart.org/art/1927.437)).
Raoul-Rochette kept the antiquities at the Bibliothèque nationale (the national library)
and died perpetual secretary of the Académie des beaux-arts (Academy of Fine Arts) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desir%C3%A9-Raoul_Rochette)).

Ingres began these graphite portraits in Rome, for money.
He wanted to be a history painter,
and once he had succeeded he drew them only as gifts for friends ([Cleveland Museum of Art](https://clevelandart.org/art/1927.437)).
Visitors in Rome knocked to ask for "the man who draws the little portraits",
and he told them "the man who lives here is a painter!"
About 450 of the drawings survive,
and they are now among his most admired works ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Auguste-Dominique_Ingres)).

He drew them on smooth wove paper,
not the ribbed laid paper that is sold today as "Ingres paper",
and one portrait took him about four hours, an hour
and a half in the morning and two
and a half in the afternoon ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Auguste-Dominique_Ingres)).
The museum points to her fashionable costume,
an open coat over a dress with leg-of-mutton sleeves,
drawn in "stark, confident lines,
with no apparent erasing or correction" ([Cleveland Museum of Art](https://clevelandart.org/art/1927.437)).
