Leonardo.
Obviously there's so much precision here.
Part of the question is, well, of course he
writes mirror image,
but that also explains the letters around that box,
for instance, the b and d are reversed.
I wonder if he's being private, perhaps.
I'm talking about the upper skull.
I think his writing is fine, beautiful.

I try to do anatomic studies daily.
All these are natural shapes that you have to kind of get in your hand,
and the more time you spend drawing these types of organic shapes,
the more practice it is.
I think that's one benefit, and the other one is that you're also learning.
For instance, the skull, I think,
is good for portraiture.
It's telling you something about what's not changing.
The thing about a portrait is that the skull is always fixed.
The jaw, of course.
Their mouth could be open or closed,
or partly, or whatever,
and they could make all kinds of expressions,
but the skull isn't going to change.
So where does it show through in a face?
I think the cheekbone, as we move off center,
the jawline, the top of the head,
the brow ridge, the nose ridge,
these remain fixed.

One could just get a photographic or computer-generated, or some other kind of, skull.
It's remarkable, the precision of this one, of course,
as a drawing,
but the other thing about this one is that it's Leonardo,
and it's an opportunity to understand his rendering.
As I look at it,
there are these defined lines that I think he built up,
and so I wonder, can I do this with a brush?
Why not try to do it in some minimal way?
So we would go around the outside.

There's another skull just below.
Perspective on these teeth is nice.
He's not known for cutting corners.
For the second one,
he's cut into the face.
I think it's just another opportunity to try to capture his shadings.
It's interesting that the square above is the one that he cuts out.
A straight-edged box on a round skull
gives the eye something to measure the depth against.
His box is flat to us,
but follows the curve above the line of the teeth,
showing perhaps some of the turn of the facial planes.

He hatches more graded light inside,
whereas I'm limited to a stark contrast, where
I can't partially shade that area.
Turning his gradient into black and white is a threshold,
as in a scanned image,
so I had to decide what to keep and what to throw away.
I was looking for the simplest and darkest values,
and the line that would convey the most of his form,
as best I could.

I guess he wanted to show what's inside of the cheekbone.
These are sinuses,
and he wanted to investigate and depict that.
I didn't know there were all these little pipes
and holes inside of the skull itself, but it makes sense.
Those little holes, I suspect, are where air
and maybe saliva or mucus flow around.
So these holes, I think, are
what caught his interest,
because they are functional.

He drew the sinus more accurately
than anyone would describe it for another 160 years,
and then explained it with the humours,
a store of fluid to feed the teeth,
and tears coming from the heart.
So what matters more, seeing or understanding?
I do think his sense of depiction was sine qua non,
and whatever the theory he was pursuing,
it was the best of the time that he was aware of,
and these mattered enough to him
to apply his time towards creating this cutaway,
to understand these points better.
Today we have a more accurate theory of what's happening biologically,
but we don't find people drawing so carefully
and drawing cut-away sections to understand and depict.

## Auction

The sheet is in the Royal Collection, RCIN 919057,
with others from the same 1489 run of skull studies (919058,
919059), and no auction record for it turned up.

## Background

The sheet is small, 18.8 × 13.4 cm, drawn in pen
and ink over traces of black chalk.
On the front Leonardo looks for the *senso comune*,
the point where the senses were thought to meet,
placing it with a grid at mid-height of the skull.
On the back, the side traced here,
the skull rests on a block under the occipital bone.
In the lower drawing he has cut away the outer wall of the eye socket
and parts of the cheekbone and upper jaw,
to show the full depth of the orbit
and the maxillary sinus behind it,
and labelled what he found with small letters,
*b* for the optic foramen, *n* for the tear duct,
and *m* for the sinus's opening into the nose,
says the [Royal Collection](https://col.rct.uk/collection/919057/recto-the-skull-sectioned-verso-the-cranium).

The drawing is accurate before the theory is.
The Royal Collection credits Nathaniel Highmore with the first description of the maxillary sinus,
in 1651, 162 years later,
while Leonardo's own notes explain it with the old theory of humours.
The sinus holds the humour that nourishes the roots of the teeth,
and tears travel to the eye from the heart.

The notes are in mirror writing, like most of his notebooks.
He was left-handed,
and "it was probably easier for him to write from right to left",
says [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci).
