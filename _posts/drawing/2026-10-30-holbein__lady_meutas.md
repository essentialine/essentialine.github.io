Another one, just those lines.
I'm using brush, and based on
[the colorful one yesterday](/drawing/holbein__the_lady_parker/),
I think it'd be more interesting to try just a non-colored one today.
And so, I think the brush gives you some variation that could be useful.

One of these hats, I think.
Is this a hat or something very fancy?
Just the basic outline there.
Holbein gives her headdress a few long lines
and spends his color on her face.
Here the priority has something to do with her face and form.

The side of her face is kind of tricky, because
I'm thinking maybe just a line is actually better than coming in heavy with the...
A brush can swell into a heavy line.
Here I did think a heavy line, especially on her longer lower face,
would not be as clean as the lines of the edge and of the ridge.

Her medallion is an oval, and in my tracing it's nearly a circle.
A circle seen at a tilt becomes an ellipse.
In this case, with the short axis vertical,
it seems to me the medallion itself is oval,
and not her angle to the viewer,
which would cause the short axis to be horizontal.
It is close to one of Holbein's own designs,
for a Mary Magdalen medallion,
and on this sheet he also drew a study of hands with rings.
Is the jewelry doing the work of a name?
Yes, I think these were meant to be associations.

Her collarbones are faint, and real.
Very hard to see those necklaces,
but they're cross-contours, and placed so well,
lying on her.
They're not just there, of course, but following her form.

His cross-contours have a certain power,
a descriptive slight angularity to them.
This angle, I think, for example on the drapery, or here,
on her right shoulder.
Why would an angular line describe her neck better than a smooth one?
Her collarbone is not round, and the necklace perfectly sets it off
and draws our eye to these levels.
Her reality, her anatomy, we see through her neck and her
slight left shoulder trapezius muscle indication.

## Auction

The sheet has never been sold.
Like the other Holbein heads at Windsor,
it passed from Henry VIII through the Earls of Arundel,
Lord Lumley and the Stuart princes,
and was back in the royal collection by 1675,
says the [Royal Collection](https://www.rct.uk/collection/912222).

<a id="background"></a>

## Background

The only name on the sheet is "The Lady Meutas",
written at the upper left in an eighteenth-century hand.
She was Joan (or Jane) Astley, who died in 1577,
sister of the London mercer John Astley.
She married the courtier Peter Meutas, who, like Holbein,
went on Henry VIII's embassies to look over possible brides ([Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hans_Holbein_the_Younger_-_Joan,_Lady_Meutas_RL_12222.jpg)).

She faces three-quarters to the left and wears a headdress,
a necklace and a pendant.
Below them Holbein outlined a medallion,
and at the right of the sheet he drew a separate study of hands with rings on the fingers ([Royal Collection](https://www.rct.uk/collection/912222)).
The design on her oval medallion is close to one of Holbein's own designs for a Mary Magdalen medallion ([Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hans_Holbein_the_Younger_-_Joan,_Lady_Meutas_RL_12222.jpg)).

From 1532 to his death Holbein drew his sitters on small sheets of paper primed pink,
and "transferred each drawn portrait study to the panel with the aid of geometrical instruments",
says [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Holbein_the_Younger).
