This is Sargent, and that is, of course, Madame Gautreau.
It's a very interesting disposition.
He did a few different works of her, watercolour too,
and obviously the famous one.
He was familiarizing himself with her.
Her arms, for instance, are important in *Madame X*.

This one has some flowers,
but I'd love to see how to trace this outstretched arm.
It's in a somewhat unusual place in terms of paintings by masters.
It looks like it's outdoors, some kind of a patio or something like that.
It's just an interesting pose,
and the command he shows of her forearm
is much higher than I was even able to trace well.

The forehead, the way he renders her forehead, is very,
very distinctive,
and I think clearly something that he wanted to capture about her.
She is in profile.
The variation of the nose and lips is maximal at the side,
and they are more distinctive than the cheekbone,
brow ridge and eye socket that form the edge of the three-quarter view.

And this one also, I think,
I'll try to see if I can do something minimal with a brush pen.
There are no outlines in the painting,
so where does the line go?
It's just the boundary of the light and dark.
Here again, because he was working directly with white versus black contrast,
the brush pen has a different line sense,
so I had to make up what it meant.

I think that might be it.
But I think the arm was just very simple,
but it's just good proportionally, and the face orientation.
And I think I was a little too thick with the lines.

## Auction

Sargent's auction record is *Group with Parasols (A Siesta)* (1905),
sold at Sotheby's New York in December 2004.
The estimate was $12 million,
and it sold for US$23.5 million.

<a id="background"></a>

## Background

Sargent painted this oil in 1882–83, on a panel 32 × 41 cm.
It is in the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum (Boston), accession P3w41.

Virginie Amélie Avegno (1859–1915) was born in New Orleans to a French Creole family
and came to France with her widowed mother in 1867.
In 1878 she married Pierre Gautreau, a banker
and shipping magnate about twenty years older.
She was known in Paris for her pale skin,
heightened with lavender powder,
and for her henna-dyed hair.

The portrait was Sargent's idea, not a commission.
She had refused many painters, and accepted him in February 1883.
In June he went to her estate in Brittany
and made about thirty studies in pencil, watercolour
and oil.
In September he wrote that he was "still at Paramé,
basking in the sunshine of my beautiful model's countenance."

The famous *Portrait de Mme \*\*\** went to the Salon of 1884.
One strap of her gown was painted slipping off the shoulder,
and the reaction was
so hostile that Sargent repainted it upright.
Her mother asked him to withdraw the picture, and he refused.
French commissions dried up, and by 1886 he had settled in London.
He sold it to the Met in 1916,
a year after her death,
calling it "the best thing I've done."
