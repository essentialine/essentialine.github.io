And then the Sargent, again this big, billowy sort of dress, this big turn.
We see, again, her anatomy just perfectly here, indirectly.
This one has even more just clarity around her knees, her hips, her torso.
Can't see her hand.
She lies back fully dressed, her dress covers her from throat to hem, and yet her knees, hips and torso read more clearly than in yesterday's seated sheet.
The indirection and the achieved sense convey the idea that her underlying form is important to Sargent.

Just her gaze, big umbrella,
of course, with the orientation as key, swirling, leading in,
really, around that handle.

Yesterday I left out the parasol's ribs and said my tracing was worse for it. 
Yes, these are a hint that Sargent employed and was able to also benefit this tracing.
So, with his watercolors, achieving so much coloration.

But for the beginning, I want to get just the basic structure down.
Again, the use of this big bonnet is, again,
the same thing in a way as the umbrella.

Her face sits inside two rings, the bonnet's brim and, behind it, the parasol.
I think it's not a halo, though it suggests one.

She lies along a long diagonal from the bonnet at top left to the hem at the bottom, and we look at her from somewhere near her feet, so her length is foreshortened.
It's an exaggeration, the largeness of her dress as she recedes.

And even this line of her breasts and waist,
it's a fairly small line,
a suggestion.
The structure, and I mean,
these markers,
capture that variation.
But, he's using colors and I will not touch that,
because I think I've already gotten quite a lot.

One short stroke has to stand for a whole torso under a loose dress.
Yes, it's about geometry and our understanding of anatomy and physiology.

And the other thing that's just remarkable is the anatomy in this.
He's making a dimensional
and also an anatomical sort of reference here.

## Auction

The sheet belongs to the Museu de Montserrat,
the museum of the thousand-year-old abbey of Montserrat in Catalonia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Montserrat)).
Its modern rooms hang the Catalans Fortuny, Rusiñol, Casas
and Nonell, then Picasso and Dalí, with Monet, Sisley, Degas
and Pissarro beside them ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Montserrat)).
A watercolour held by a museum has no sale price to report.

## Background

John Singer Sargent was born in Florence in 1856 to American parents,
and died at his home in Chelsea in 1925.
He made roughly 900 oil paintings
and more than 2,000 watercolours ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Singer_Sargent)).
As a young man, after his training in Paris,
he went to Spain
and studied Velázquez with a passion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Singer_Sargent)).

Watercolour was where Sargent painted for himself.
Away from the pressures of the portrait studio he painted friends
and family resting in bright landscapes,
with a more vivid palette
and freer handling than his commissions allowed ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Singer_Sargent)).
This sheet is 53 by 40 centimetres,
watercolour on paper ([Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:John_Singer_Sargent_-_Dame_mit_Sonnenschirm_-1900.jpeg)).
No source I have found names the woman.

Watercolour is pigment carried in water,
laid on paper ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watercolor_painting)).
A transparent wash can darken the paper
but never lighten it,
so the brightest whites are the paper itself, left bare.
Here she lies back on the ground under a white parasol,
in a wide white bonnet with a blue scarf at her throat,
and her white dress fills the lower two-thirds of the sheet in long strokes of lilac,
blue and rose, with the paper showing through for the sun.
Her face is small and exact.
