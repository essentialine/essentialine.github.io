Munch's *Døden i sykeværelset* (Death in the Sickroom).
It's an extraordinary work. It's kind of fundamental to me,
for me it tells me what art is about. It reminds me
of why are we doing this,
what he's trying to get on paper and express, and what he achieves
through and for me.
I've never been in this kind of a room,
but I understand this and him and myself when I see it.
The first time I saw it I understood this painting instantly,
and I was drawn to it,
because it's not often that one understands another person
the way this allows that immediate connection.

What makes that possible?
Its history and circumstance and emotion.
These are situations and thoughts that happen in my dreams,
and that I understand and relate to.

One of the main figures in the painting.
I wanted to focus on her and her dress with these spots.
I don't think I'll draw the spots, but I will draw her face,
and the people around her.

Compared with his,
I think mine has a different way of connecting and distinguishing
the figures at the left. His are blending in dark colours and
tones, but mine are separate in their silhouettes, and then
the same in the sense they're all lines of equal width and colour.

The colours are merging in many places,
but there's also a distinct set of lines.
Even though it's merging, it's not totally merging.
At her collar, these lines of the hair are quite interesting.
This could be shadow or form.
We're getting this line, so I'm feeling okay to trace this one.

Munch came back to this room in prints.
I think he does those prints because his forms and figures
are amenable to it, which is also why it was sort of easy for me.

I need to draw their hair
and some of the furniture in the background.
One problem I'm having is that his work with shadows is tricky.
There's a shadow of the far figure against the wall,
a painting up here, and the desk with books, which also has shadows.
I think this is a shadow of the girl in the foreground
onto the central woman.
I'll add her far arm. Her hair is important here.
The man in the back is
looking towards us.
This shadow below him could work, I'll try it.
You'd need the bottom of this wall in that case.
The pinkish thing at the bottom may be some kind of moulding.
A little of the desk stuff here. The painting on the wall.

I don't think this one's meaning lives in its shadows, and I did
get the far man's shadow, which looks very much like the sunlight
in [*Stemmen*](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Edvard_Munch_-_Summer_Night%27s_Dream._The_Voice_(1893).jpg) (The Voice), that kind of [sun with a line coming down](/drawing/munch-livets-dans-line-and-video/),
so in that sense there may be dark meaning there of sorrow.
But here I think the arrangement itself carries a lot of meaning.

The eyes.
I don't think the almond eyes are entirely incorrect.
They are masklike, which seems quite appropriate. The far man's
eye also seems incongruous like Munch's, though perhaps less
clearly.

I'm glad to finish in a way
that I think is the right place to stop.

<a id="notebook"></a>

**Munch wrote his sister's last hour** in an 1890 note now in the
Munchmuseet archive, MM N 3670. He tells it as fiction, the dying girl
is called Maja, and the [Nasjonalmuseet](https://www.nasjonalmuseet.no/en/collection/object/NG.M.00940) quotes it beside this
picture. She points to the armchair, the one she sits in here
with her back to us.

![Facsimile of Munch's manuscript note MM N 3670, page 4, on the dying girl and the armchair](/assets/images/1893_munch__doden_i_sykevaerelset/1893_munch__doden_i_sykevaerelset-mm_n3670-4-facsimile.jpg)
*MM N 3670, page 4, Munchmuseet archive. The passage is the lower half. © Munch Museum, Oslo, via [eMunch.no](https://emunch.no/HYBRIDNo-MM_N3670.xhtml), where the facsimile and transcription sit side by side.*

> *Skulde hun da virkeli dø – … i den sidste halve time følte hun sig jo næsten lættere … – hun forsøgt at reise sig op – pekte på lænestolen der stod ved siden – Jeg vil op … prøve at sidde oppi hvisket hun –*
>
> "Should she really die – in the last half hour she felt almost lighter – She tried to raise herself – pointed to the armchair that stood beside her – I would like to sit up she whispered …"

<small>Translation from the [Nasjonalmuseet's catalogue](https://www.nasjonalmuseet.no/en/collection/object/NG.M.00940). The Norwegian follows eMunch's transcription, cut to match.</small>

<a id="background"></a>

**Background.** Munch painted *Døden i sykeværelset* in 1893, oil on
canvas, 134 × 160 cm, and it is in the Munchmuseet, Oslo
([Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Edvard_Munch_-_Death_in_the_Sickroom_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg)).
It depicts the death of his sister Sophie, which he re-worked in many
later variations, and, in the words of Wikipedia, "portrays his entire
family" with its drama "dispersed in the separate and disconnected
figures of sorrow"
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edvard_Munch)).
Johanne Sophie Munch, his favourite sister, died of tuberculosis in
1877 at fifteen. Their mother had died of the same disease in 1868
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edvard_Munch)).

There are two main versions, both dated 1893. The other, in tempera
and oil crayon, is in the [Nasjonalmuseet](https://www.nasjonalmuseet.no/en/collection/object/NG.M.00940), Oslo (NG.M.00940),
and there has been disagreement about which came first.
Its catalogue names the family. Sophie sits in the chair with her back
to us and aunt Karen Bjølstad rests a hand on it. The father, the
doctor Christian Munch, stands behind with his hands clasped.
[Inger](/drawing/munch-inger-alexandra-line-and-oil/#inger) is the
woman staring straight at us,
[Laura](/drawing/munch-laura-line-and-video/) sits in the foreground,
Edvard is probably the man in quarter-face near the centre,
and his brother Andreas stands at the left.
"Although they are together," the catalogue says,
"each has to grapple with his or her grief alone."
Munch came back to the scene in a lithograph in 1896
([Nasjonalmuseet](https://www.nasjonalmuseet.no/en/collection/object/NG.M.00940)).

<img src="/assets/images/1893_munch__doden_i_sykevaerelset/1893_munch__doden_i_sykevaerelset-nasjonalmuseet_version.jpg" alt="The Nasjonalmuseet version of Munch's Death in the Sickroom, paler, with the family around the chair" style="display: block; max-width: 300px; margin: 1.5rem auto;">
*Edvard Munch,* Døden i sykeværelset *(Death in the Sickroom), 1893, tempera and oil crayon on canvas, 152.5 × 169.5 cm — [Nasjonalmuseet](https://www.nasjonalmuseet.no/en/collection/object/NG.M.00940), Oslo, NG.M.00940. Photo: Børre Høstland, Nasjonalmuseet. Public domain.*

In a 1918 note on the Frieze of Life, MM N 36, Munch lists
*Sygeværelset* among the pictures he was at last, disheartened, forced
to sell, to Rasmus Meyer and the National Gallery in Kristiania
([eMunch.no](https://emunch.no/HYBRIDNo-MM_N0036.xhtml)).
