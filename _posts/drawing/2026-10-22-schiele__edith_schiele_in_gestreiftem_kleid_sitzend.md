This is Schiele's wife Edith.
She lived across the street from him,
and I think he married her instead of [Wally]({{ site.baseurl }}/drawing/schiele__der_tod_und_das_madchen/).
And her face is similar but different.
You can tell that this is not Wally.

I chose this one because
there's a lot of texture and interest
and everything,
but I specifically liked to look for some clothing that
still conveys something anatomical,
like the implicit and the explicit, right?
So here,
I feel like there's just a great sense of the shoulders,
the posture, and the face.
Generally a clothed figure cannot teach anatomy,
but only reflect it.
The fact that this is so convincing
arises because his anatomical perception is accurate.

And then of course the dress,
with these real lines on it that really help you.
This one is fairly novel
in that the stripes are so directly rendered.
You can see this form through this line thing,
almost in a coordinate fashion.
The striped cloth was a curtain first.
I think he understood immediately
the lines would give a striking
and immediate 3D rendition of form.
He's got a tremendous amount of information here through the drapery,
and it's a way of saying something about the situation.

I've got the eyes and the eyebrows and the outline.
Of course her hands.
His lines always are communicative and informative,
and with just this much we have a lot of the volume.
Her dress especially.

I feel the lines of her dress are most educational to add.
This one has a kink to it, this one is straighter.
I might start with the straighter one,
because I'm just trying to see how much does even one of these lines tell us.
One problem, perhaps, is that there's a fold involved.
The fold is almost better,
as it is a way to convey even a crosscutting shape.
The lines are similar to what they use in computer graphics,
and a natural mathematical description of a complex 3D shape.

Using a brush can help with getting some of this variation in.
But it might not be as sharp.
His stripes also swell and thin,
so it was a good match.
These lines have almost a bony feeling to them.
I mean the shapes of the stripes themselves
have that muscle memory of Schiele's sense of bone and anatomy,
and perhaps also are indirectly reflective of her skeletal anatomy.

Now I think it is worth putting in some of these lines of the sleeves
because we do need to know where her sleeves are against the stripes.
Her jacket has this textural lumpiness.
But if we wanted to think about the paint,
it's a warm sort of a coat that she's wearing,
conveyed with these brush strokes.

## Auction

This sheet has been to auction once.
At Sotheby's London on 30 June 1992, lot 25,
it carried an estimate of £300,000–400,000
and sold to the collector Rudolf Leopold for a hammer price of £520,000.
The consignor had owned it since 1936. Elisabeth Bunzl,
of the Viennese paper family Bunzl & Biach,
asked for it as her wedding present,
took it with her when she fled to England,
and kept it for 56 years.

## Background

Edith Harms and her sister Adéle
lived with their parents
across the street from Schiele's studio in Wien.
In 1915 he chose to marry Edith,
the more socially acceptable match,
and expected to keep seeing Wally Neuzil, his model
and partner. Wally left as soon as he told her,
and never saw him again.
They married on Wednesday 17 June 1915,
and three days later he was ordered to report for active service.
He drew Edith that year from above, in the striped dress,
looking out of the picture somewhat hesitantly,
as the Leopold puts it.
The dress was a curtain first.
The museum's provenance dossier notes
that the striped cloth hangs as a curtain
in an earlier still life of his desk.

The stripes are what make the drawing work as anatomy.
Each one is a line laid on the cloth,
and as the cloth goes over a knee or into a lap the line bends with it,
the way the grid lines on a map bend over a hill in a relief drawing.
Read together they are a set of coordinates over her body.
Where they run straight, the surface is flat,
and where they crowd and kink, it turns away from us or folds.
So a seated woman in a long,
loose dress still shows the thigh, the knee
and the drop of the skirt to the floor.

Edith became the model for most of Schiele's women after 1915.
In the autumn of 1918, six months pregnant,
she died of the Spanish flu on 28 October,
and Schiele died three days later, on 31 October, aged 28.
Wally had died the December before, of scarlet fever, at 23.
Her side of the story is in [*Tod und Mädchen*]({{ site.baseurl }}/drawing/schiele__der_tod_und_das_madchen/).
