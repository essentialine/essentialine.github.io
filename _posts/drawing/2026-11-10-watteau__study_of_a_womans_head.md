So, a second Watteau.
Her eyebrow or eyelid is very nice,
and there's a harmoniousness,
like the lines have this sort of motion to them that's very harmonious,
and very sort of anatomically true, I think.
It is the simplicity, the bone structure, expressivity,
the aliveness and realism.

Her nose ridge is so straight, and then angled at the tip,
but it reminds me a little bit of Loomis,
the studied and sort of mannequin look,
but somehow still more alive.

Loomis is good at making a complex shape approachable,
but then they all have a certain mannequin look.
This has a bit of that face's shape, but more alive.

There's a lot of just very nice form here in her cheekbone
and cheek and all that, and that same hair technique of
the strands not a hairline as a line.

Her hair is naturally drawn lines.

Here Watteau's hatching was far superior for the shading of her cheek.
My wash was too crude, but it could work with some more fineness.

Looking away, indirection, can show other parts of the form,
and facial features like the eyes can be very expressive.
Here they can be balanced off with other forms.

## Auction

The sheet came to the Hermitage in 1923 from the library of the Stieglitz Museum in Petrograd,
and it carries the museum's inventory number 40024 ([Commons,
Artwork template](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Antoine_Watteau,_Study_of_a_Woman%27s_Head_(1710s).jpg)).
Watteau's chalk heads were collected in his own lifetime,
even by men who found fault with his paintings,
the comte de Caylus
and the dealer Gersaint among them ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Watteau)).

## Background

Jean-Antoine Watteau was baptised in Valenciennes in October 1684
and died in July 1721, at 36.
In Paris he copied Flemish
and Dutch genre pictures in a workshop at the Pont Notre-Dame,
and there he developed his sketchlike technique.
Working later for Claude Audran III,
curator of the Palais du Luxembourg,
he could study the canvases Rubens painted there for Marie de' Medici,
and Rubens became one of his major influences ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Watteau)).

This head is drawn *aux trois crayons*, in three chalks,
black, red (*sanguine*) and white,
on a light brown paper ([Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Antoine_Watteau,_Study_of_a_Woman%27s_Head_(1710s).jpg)).
The paper is toned
because white chalk barely shows on white,
and the technique itself came to Watteau from Rubens,
who pioneered
and popularised it ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trois_crayons)).

A mid-tone paper is a small machine for *chiaroscuro*, light
and dark used to give a figure volume ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiaroscuro)).
The paper does the middle tones for free,
so the artist draws only the two ends of the scale,
white chalk up into the light
and black chalk down into the shadow.
In this sheet the black is hatched, in the hair,
under her jaw and down her neck,
and the red is laid on her cheek, lips and ear.
