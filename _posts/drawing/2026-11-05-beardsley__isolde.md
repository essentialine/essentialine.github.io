Interesting picture.
A little hard to see her face from here, but fine.
A lot of lines here, and the proportion is so exaggerated.
It's something,
to be able to express by distorting proportion, which we are
so finely tuned to.

The stretch is in the service of an expression. It is elegance, regalness, gracefulness.

Her face is the smallest and hardest thing to see. We can see her profile face, but through the tracing paper it was hard.

And especially with the quality of this… We have this complicated
part of her hat.
Starting with the silhouette, and see how much that goes,
because he does have internal lines,
but for example on her dress,
her sleeves… Down here at the bottom of her dress,
you can clearly see… this line is vertical.

Beardsley's design is already close to a silhouette at heart, like a Japanese print or a shadow puppet. It's already kind of a silhouette, but with the hair and hat filled in.

It's funny that he chose this vertical line that is kind of our closest point.
This line isn't off to the side or something,
it's very close to where our line of sight is.
It's edge on,
and there is some line variation here that is giving it that depth.
Just depth cueing stuff.

A surface seen edge on becomes a straight line, and that is why the fold nearest us is so straight. Here it's a less direct way of conveying the form of her dress, since it's right edge on us. A side line or cross line might give more information. Again he's using indirection and suggestion. The dress itself is hiding her form, and the contour we get hides the form of the dress.

A line block can print only solid ink or bare paper, with no greys. The blocks and lines are similar in the sense that they control the edge. They differ in that the block conveys the emotional content of the interior in a single color.

## Auction

*Isolde* was printed for every reader of *The Studio*,
so it is a common sheet rather than a rare proof.
I found no auction house result for it to quote.

## Background

Isolde is the heroine of the medieval romance of Tristan
and Isolde, an adulterous love triangle.
Beardsley knew her best through Wagner's opera *Tristan und Isolde*,
which he loved ([V&A](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O901567/)).
He shows her before a red curtain,
about to drink from a cup she believes holds poison.
It holds a love potion.

The print was not made for a book. *The Studio*,
the London art journal,
gave it to its readers as a colour supplement in October 1895 (volume VI,
issue 31),
beside an article on the Herkomer School ([V&A](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O901567/),
[Cleveland Museum of Art](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/2010.651)).
It was printed in two processes,
a line block for the drawing
and colour lithography for the flat colour,
by William Griggs.

Beardsley was famous in the 1890s for a "stark, sinuous,
and linear style", and he died of tuberculosis in 1898,
at 25 ([Cleveland Museum of Art](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/2010.651)).
