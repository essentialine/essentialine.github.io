There's also a little bit of hatching,
which I wonder if a brush pen could convey the form of hatching.

A contour-only head keeps some of Dürer and loses the hatching, which
the burin cut on copper plates and the chalk lays down here. Whether a
brush can do hatching at all is the question I can't settle. They are
very different ways of conveying the form, with different emotional
and aesthetic implications.

On this sheet the paper is the middle tone, and the white chalk has
mostly rubbed away, so on my white tracing sheet the paper is all light.

I search for a way to add light and chiaroscuro, which a toned medium
makes possible, but here Dürer wasn't using that.
Perhaps it adds an atmosphere, a blending of the subject and the ground.

I kept the ear in some detail and left most of the hair, and the reason
is what each one shows about the head. The ear is a more fundamental
thing in some ways than the hair.
Hair can be in a knot or in other shapes, whereas the ear is indicative
of something more fixed about the orientation of the skull.

The question is whether the features fall where his rules put them,
and how the turn foreshortens the far side. These do fall very
realistically, and the turn is convincing and subtle, so it's quite
clear that he was expert in this.

The brush pen's line swells and thins with pressure, and that changes how
her contours read, the jaw especially. Brush pens are very good at
quickly laying down variations in line, and so they can be good for
form, but one needs to study where variation and form can convey
interesting information.

The simplicity of the line is how [Frédéric Forest](https://fredericforest.com) draws,
and he sets it out in *Art of the Line*, his teaching book.

## Background

Dürer drew this head in black chalk on paper he had first coated green,
then picked out the light with white chalk, most of
which has since rubbed away ([The Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/459211)).
Coloured paper was a habit he brought back from Venice,
where in 1505–1507 he made his preparatory drawings on blue paper ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albrecht_D%C3%BCrer)).

The year before, he had travelled through the Netherlands,
drawing in silverpoint, chalk and charcoal in Antwerp,
and seeing van Eyck's Ghent Altarpiece.
He came home to Nuremberg in July 1521 with an illness that stayed with him for the rest of his life
and slowed his work ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albrecht_D%C3%BCrer)).

In those last years he gave much of his time to geometry and proportion.
His *Underweysung der Messung* (Instructions for Measuring),
1525,
was the first book for adults on mathematics in German,
later cited by Galileo and Kepler,
and his *Four Books on Human Proportion* came out in 1528,
shortly after his death ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albrecht_D%C3%BCrer)).
