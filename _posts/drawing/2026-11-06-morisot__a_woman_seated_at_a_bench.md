Berthe Morisot, beautiful watercolor style that I haven't looked at as much.
These blotches, I think, are done just right.
And to convey this, there's people back here and whatnot.
This line of the ridge of the mountain, I think,
is kind of all you really need, in a way.

And I think she might have had some kind of a line here,
or just a fine brush, for some of her earlier steps of this.
Watercolor has almost no drawn edges,
so to trace it you have to decide which edges exist.
I did think she used a very fine brush, or some kind of underlay.
Lots of green in the background.

These speckles on her dress suggest form.
They have that property of the kind of speckled light
but these are not ordinary speckles that are randomly placed to fill in the color.
When we trace them, we can see that they're very specifically chosen.
The question is what a blot does that a line can't,
and how her placed touch differs from Monet's broken color
or Seurat's dots, which follow a system and not the eye.
Hers seem very intuitive, and Monet's are also,
but these have a kind of shape which is expressive.

The parasol fills most of the right half,
almost a second figure turned toward her.
The question is how it balances her,
and how that compares with Degas or Cassatt
cropping and offsetting their figures in the same years.
She is quite evenly balanced with the umbrella, I think.
Later there was more freedom of balance.

I could brush for variation, or draw lines for cleanliness.
I pick the colored Microns.
Watercolor is transparent, so the white paper is part of the light,
and a tracing has to turn that into colored line.
I do think colors are expressive, and edge detection is an important
aspect of vision, a kind of dual in the mathematical sense, perhaps.

So then, I guess the other question is,
can we use a brush to fill in any larger swaths of this?
Of course… and this could be it, perhaps just leave, this time, with lines.
A tracing keeps where things are, the parasol's ellipse
and its ribs meeting at one point, but it can't measure
the width of a brushmark or how wet it was.
The grid can help with specific larger constructs,
but the finer details can't work that way,
and those often breathe life and freedom.

## Auction

This sheet has been at the Met since 1948, so it has no price of its own.
The nearest comparable I found is a sheet of her daughter,
*Julie Manet à la table* (Julie Manet at the table), pencil
and watercolour from 1886 and almost the same size,
25.1 × 30.5 cm.
It sold at Bonhams in New York on 7 July 2020 for **US$11,950** with premium ([Bonhams,
lot 27](https://www.bonhams.com/auction/26153/lot/27/berthe-morisot-1841-1895-julie-manet-a-la-table-recto-etude-des-tetes-verso-9-78-x-12-in-251-x-305-cm-executed-in-1886/)).

## Background

The Avenue du Bois de Boulogne, today's Avenue Foch,
was Haussmann's grand approach to the Bois, 120 metres wide
and lined with chestnut trees, built
so that fashionable Parisians could ride out to the park to see
and be seen ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avenue_Foch)).
Morisot puts a woman on a bench at its edge,
a blue parasol open beside her,
with a strip of promenaders on the far side.

Morisot learned watercolour outdoors, following Corot,
because it was easy to carry,
and critics described her touch with the verb *effleurer* (to brush lightly against) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berthe_Morisot)).
She showed with the Impressionists from their first exhibition in 1874,
the only woman in it, and missed only one of the eight.

1885, the year of this sheet, is where her Wikipedia biography marks a turn.
After it drawing came to dominate her work,
with more attention to clear form and line,
and from Japanese prints she took the habit of placing things away from the centre.
From 1887 she used squaring
and tracing paper to transfer her drawings exactly to canvas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berthe_Morisot)).

The Met bought the sheet in 1948 through the Harris Brisbane Dick Fund ([The Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/337864)).
