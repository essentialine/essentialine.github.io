<a id="tracing"></a>

So this is Watteau.
I thought her features were anatomic and balanced,
obviously with some kind of chalk or crayon,
here I use brush pen.

I think what I like about this one is just her anatomy,
and also this use of colour I think is nice.

I wondered what I kept of Watteau's colour once it had to become black on white,
and what a line drawing loses or finds when warmth and shadow are no longer separate colours.
She has tonality.
Colour is another dimension, warmth, but we give up simplicity.

And her hair, I think,
he doesn't show a line for her hairline,
it's these individual lines showing direction and mass.

Watteau stops his strokes short of the forehead, so the edge of the hair is where the strokes end, not a line drawn round it.
The edge of the texture is often very helpful to depict, and here we see that's sufficient and elegant.

She looks up and away, in a study made for no portrait and no sitter's vanity, so her face keeps a privacy that a face meeting our eyes gives up.
Here she has a candidness. We might be looking down from an opera house row at a stranger.

Mirrored drawings often look subtly wrong to the artist who made them,
and a reflected three-quarter head changes both its geometry and the way we see it.
It's a tilted side view, and it helps to practice the 3D form, to work with it from different angles.

A chalk mark sits on the tooth of the paper and breaks up where the paper rises. A brush mark lies wet on top and does not, so the two shadings read differently at the scale of the grain.
Here I saw sections of the form which seemed like they would fit the shape of the brush.

## Auction

Watteau's chalk drawings were collected in his own lifetime,
even by men who found fault with his paintings,
the comte de Caylus and the dealer Gersaint among them,
and in 1726 and 1728
Jean de Jullienne published suites of etchings after them ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Watteau)).

<a id="background"></a>

## Background

Jean-Antoine Watteau was baptised in Valenciennes in October 1684
and died in July 1721, at 36,
perhaps of tuberculous laryngitis.
He is credited with inventing the *fête galante*,
and his *Pilgrimage to Cythera* was the reception piece that made him a full member of the Academy in 1717 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Watteau)).

In Paris, working for the decorator Claude Audran III,
curator of the Palais du Luxembourg,
he could study the canvases Rubens painted there for Marie de' Medici,
and Rubens became one of his major influences ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Watteau)).

The technique of this family of sheets came from Rubens too. *Trois crayons*,
three chalks, puts red (*sanguine*), black
and white on a mid-tone paper, grey, blue or tan,
because white chalk barely shows on white paper.
Rubens pioneered and popularised it,
and Watteau drew his studies of heads
and figures with it ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trois_crayons)).
The Met catalogues this sheet as red and white chalk and graphite
on off-white laid paper, so the dark lines in the hair are pencil,
not the black chalk of the full set. The red is on her cheek and ear
([Commons, Artwork template](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Study_of_a_Woman%E2%80%99s_Head_and_Hands_MET_DP827485.jpg)).
