It's curious that nobody knows who Lady Parker is.
This one is almost minimal in its own way.
There's not a lot of shading, and the shading that's there,
there's a tiny bit here on the left side of her face,
but her gaze is just extraordinary, her outfit,
the whole image.
Holbein drew her on paper primed pink,
so does the ground stand in for her skin?
Well I think the lines are the only carriers of information here.
To me such a toned ground is ground,
and thus his drawing is minimal,
not that he matched her skin color.

I use brush pens and some color.
One thing is to just take the line itself,
the other is to maybe build something over it.
I think maybe I'll start with a black brush
and see how that feels, and start minimal,
and see if we want to build anything over it.
Just the outline.
His drawings were working patterns,
carried to the panel with geometrical instruments,
maybe even a tracing device, and I'm tracing too.
I do think the devices also have a good role.
I have the [NeoLucida XL](https://www.bigidesign.com/products/neolucida-xl),
which may be in a similar direction,
but here it's not about the devices but the source,
which is very powerful.

Her eyes are widely spaced.
They're very expressive of the form,
and even of something else, this feeling of hers.
The rule of thumb is one eye's width between her eyes.
She's clearly wider than that rule of thumb,
and beautiful, powerful, simple, calm.
She barely has eyebrows, and her lips look better,
I can barely make out.

So now for the lines of her dress, back to this black brush.
These lines are not exactly cross contour lines.
And her collar is just so, almost casual.
To me that's surprising.
It feels like she is an aristocrat,
and I'm sure you get that sense somehow from her.
With no name to go on, where does it come from?
Just her calmness, her attire, her collar open.
I think this line here across the chest of her dress is beautiful.
Next to the dress in Ingres's
[study for the Comtesse d'Haussonville](/drawing/ingres__etude_pour_la_comtesse_d_haussonville/),
it's different.
For me this handling of her dress is more compact,
a few lines with such descriptiveness.

Now I use some light brushes.
Some of this light shading goes over this, but I think
in the end his image, a few lines, was far more informationally compact,
and colors perhaps are slightly distracting
from where I was with just the black brush.

## Auction

The sheet has never been sold.
It passed from Henry VIII through the Earls of Arundel,
Lord Lumley and the Stuart princes,
and was back in the royal collection by 1675,
says the [Royal Collection](https://www.rct.uk/collection/912230).

<a id="background"></a>

## Background

The only name on the sheet is "The Lady Parker",
written at the upper left in an eighteenth-century hand,
long after Holbein died in London in 1543.
The [Royal Collection](https://www.rct.uk/collection/912230) takes her to be Grace,
daughter of Sir John Newport, who married Sir Henry Parker,
heir to Lord Morley, in 1523, when she was about eight,
and was probably one of Jane Seymour's ladies.
K. T. Parker, cataloguing the Windsor drawings in 1945,
ruled out Jane Parker, Lady Rochford,
and thought Grace the likelier of Sir Henry's two wives "if the inscription is reliable" ([Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Grace,_Lady_Parker_by_Hans_Holbein_the_Younger.jpg)).
A 2024 article proposes someone else altogether,
Susanna Horenbout,
a miniaturist at the Tudor court ([*Royal Studies Journal*](https://rsj.winchester.ac.uk/articles/467)).

Her rounded French hood
and high-collared dress are the fashion of the 1540s,
so the drawing is probably one of Holbein's last.
She looks straight out at us,
which the Royal Collection calls unusual for Holbein,
and no painted portrait made from the drawing is known.

From 1532 to his death Holbein drew his sitters on small sheets of paper primed pink,
and "transferred each drawn portrait study to the panel with the aid of geometrical instruments",
says [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Holbein_the_Younger).
