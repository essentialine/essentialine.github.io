Just a striking picture.
Her eyes, this is about gaze and people.
There are people in the far distance there.
This is meant to communicate with the viewer in a direct way.
There are works of Schiele that look out at you,
da Vinci, Sargent looks out and you get that sense.

She looks straight out at us, and so does the man in the canoe.
Connection.
And there's a man behind her involved too,
the flag on her boat behind her and in front of him,
between the two of them.
You can see the flagpole back there on her boat.
And she's holding a little rope.
And then a tree off on the shore.

The flag sits between two depths,
her in the stern and him some way back in his canoe.
It's not flat. The flag is trailing away from us in the wind.
I think their faces and boats tell us more about their distance
than the flag. The flag is a kind of intermediary.
Some kind of a symbol, it reminds me of Munch.
There's something about that flag that is symbolic, allegorical,
connection, with this sitter perhaps.

Her hat, of course, one thinks sometimes of a halo.
Her straw hat makes a bright round frame behind and around her head.
I don't think it was an important part,
although it is quite centered on her gaze.

Later owners renamed the picture *La Barque au drapeau*,
the boat with the flag, as if it were the subject.
It's informal as a Sunday boating scene, but also something else,
that it is triangular, behind her,
and even geometrically between her and the man.
I don't think it's nationalistic.

Even with just this silhouette,
the power of this work is already there.
All this color and technical
and decorative value here,
but there's something also that's elemental
to this depiction here that survives removing all of it,
right?
Like I've got practically nothing.

Renoir's water and flag are built from broken touches of colour
that only resolve at a distance.
If we can get some of the meaning and message and feeling of the original,
then it tells us the structure of the original has an elementality,
and the colors are a different portrayal of something simpler.

## Auction

*En Canot* comes up at Sotheby's New York on Wednesday 18 November 2026,
as lot 8 of *Legendary*,
the evening sale of the Blaquier-Arrieta collection.
The estimate is $25 million to $35 million,
and the lot is guaranteed ([Sotheby's](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/auction/2026/legendary-the-blaquier-arrieta-collection-evening-auction/pierre-auguste-renoir-en-canot-or-jeune-fille-a-la)).

The present owners bought it from the Lefevre Gallery in 1977.
Before that it belonged to Mr and Mrs Albert D.
Lasker of New York, who bought it from Paul Rosenberg in November 1944.
Its first owner, about 1880,
was Charles Édouard Haviland of Paris and Limoges,
who had it from Renoir himself ([Sotheby's](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/auction/2026/legendary-the-blaquier-arrieta-collection-evening-auction/pierre-auguste-renoir-en-canot-or-jeune-fille-a-la)).

## Background

Pierre-Auguste Renoir was born in Limoges in 1841
and died at Cagnes-sur-Mer in 1919 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Auguste_Renoir)).
The first owner of this picture came from the other great trade of Limoges.
Charles Haviland, born in New York in 1839,
ran his father's porcelain firm there ([Wikipédia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Edward_Haviland)).

The painting is oil on canvas, 73.7 by 92.9 centimetres,
signed Renoir
and dated 77 at the lower right ([Sotheby's](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/auction/2026/legendary-the-blaquier-arrieta-collection-evening-auction/pierre-auguste-renoir-en-canot-or-jeune-fille-a-la)).
A young woman in a dark dress
and a straw hat sits in the stern of a boat
with the tiller ropes in her hands
and looks straight out at us.
Behind her a big red, white
and blue flag hangs from a staff,
and behind the flag a man in a striped jersey
paddles a red canoe towards us.
The picture has gone by many names.
It was *En canot* at Durand-Ruel in 1920,
*La Barque à Argenteuil* in Paris in 1937,
*La Barque au drapeau* (the boat with the flag) in San Francisco in 1954,
and *Girl in a Boat* on the cover of the Art Institute of Chicago's Renoir catalogue in 1973 ([Sotheby's](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/auction/2026/legendary-the-blaquier-arrieta-collection-evening-auction/pierre-auguste-renoir-en-canot-or-jeune-fille-a-la)).

Pictures like this one depend on a piece of mid-century chemistry and tin.
Premixed paint sold in tin tubes, like toothpaste,
let the Impressionists work outdoors, *en plein air*,
and catch the passing effects of sunlight on water ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impressionism)).
Four years after this canvas Renoir painted a whole party of boaters,
*Le Déjeuner des canotiers* (Luncheon of the Boating Party),
1880–81,
now in the Phillips Collection in Washington ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Auguste_Renoir)).

Paul Rosenberg, the Paris dealer of Picasso, Braque
and Matisse, fled France
and opened a gallery at 79 East 57th Street in New York in 1940.
The Nazi looting office took 162 of his paintings from a bank vault in Bordeaux ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Rosenberg_(art_dealer))). *En Canot* went with his business from Paris to New York,
and he sold it there to Albert Lasker,
the man who did much to shape modern advertising ([Sotheby's](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/auction/2026/legendary-the-blaquier-arrieta-collection-evening-auction/pierre-auguste-renoir-en-canot-or-jeune-fille-a-la),
[Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Lasker)).
